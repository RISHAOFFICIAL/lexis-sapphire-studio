Your Client's Website Is Costing Them Fans (And How to Fix It)

Fans do not leave a bad artist website because the music is bad. They leave because the site made them work for it.

Here is the test. Open your client's website on your phone, start a timer, and try to find three things: the new album, the next tour date, and one piece of merch. If it takes more than thirty seconds, the site is costing your client fans.

The worst part is that nobody tells you. Fans do not email to say your website was confusing. They just leave, and the streams, the ticket sales, and the merch orders never happen. These are the five symptoms we see most.

1. The site is a store with nothing else

Open the site and what do you see? A grid of products and a checkout button. No music, no story, no tour dates, no videos. The artist's whole career reduced to a shop. A store is fine as one page of a website. As the entire website, it tells a fan the artist has nothing to say.

The fix is a real homepage that leads with the music and the moment. The Maya Sol example opens with the new album, Velvet Hours, the release date, the lead single with its 11 million streams, and the tour announcement, all above the fold. The shop is there, six items, but it is one section of a site that also holds the tracklist, the videos, the press, and the story. A fan who came to buy a hoodie finds a record to love.

2. The tour dates are buried or missing

A fan hears about a show, opens the site, and cannot find the date. Or finds it three clicks deep. Or finds last year's dates still up and no new ones. Every one of those is a lost ticket. Tour dates belong on the homepage and on their own page, with every city, every venue, and the status right there.

Look at how The Velvet Static example does it. Twelve cities, December 4 through February 6, each row with the day, the date, the venue, and the status. The Detroit hometown show is marked sold out, which tells Detroit fans they missed it and tells every other city that this band sells rooms out. The Maya Sol tour page does the same with ten cities and a sold out homecoming.

3. There is no story, so there is nothing to share

A journalist needs a story. A promoter needs a story. A brand needs a story. And a fan needs a reason to care beyond one song. If the site has no bio, or a bio that says nothing, all of those people move on.

The Dana Cole example shows what a story page should hold. Fourteen screen credits, an awards shelf, a now filming block, three ventures, and a bio with a timeline from her first role at nine to founding her production company in 2021. The Maya Sol story has the same shape: the church choir, the record shop on Woodward Avenue, the first paid gig at nineteen, the home recorded debut, the sold out hall in 2024. Specifics are what get quoted, what get booked, and what get remembered.

4. The merch has no context

A tee on a white background with a size selector is not merch, it is inventory. Fans buy merch because it means something: the tour they went to, the album they love. If the site does not connect the product to the music, it is just a shirt.

The Maya Sol shop ties every item to the record. The plum vinyl comes with a lyric booklet. The tour poster lists all ten dates. The candle is named for Rose Water, a track on the album. The Velvet Static shop does the same: the tour poster is a numbered run of 200, and the pin set carries the band's flower and lightning bolt.

5. It looks like every other artist site

This is the hardest one to see, because it feels normal. The same layout, the same big photo, the same three links, the same template everyone else bought. Fans have seen it a hundred times and scroll past it without thinking. Promoters have seen it too, and it tells them nothing about your artist.

A custom site looks like the artist it represents. Maya Sol's site is warm and editorial, built around her album and her Detroit story. The Velvet Static site is dark and loud, built around the band and the tour. Dana Cole's site is calm and gallery like, built around her film work and her ventures. Three artists, three completely different sites, and you could not mistake one for another.

The bottom line

If your client's site has two or more of these symptoms, patching it will not help. The structure is the problem, not the decoration. A rebuild that puts the music, the tour, the merch, the story, and the press in one place is ready in 2 to 4 weeks.
