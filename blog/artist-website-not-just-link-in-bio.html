Why Your Artist Needs a Real Website, Not Just a Link in Bio

A link in bio sends fans somewhere else. A real website keeps them with your artist, around the clock.

Open your artist's Instagram profile and tap the link in bio. For most artists, it goes to a page of links, and every one sends the fan somewhere else: Spotify for the music, a ticket site for the tour, a store for the merch, YouTube for the videos. Most fans will not bother piecing your artist together.

That is the problem with a link in bio. It is a hallway with doors to other people's platforms. A real website is a home. Everything lives in one place, under your artist's name, and every page gives a fan one more reason to stay.

1. Social platforms decide who sees your artist

Instagram, TikTok, and every other platform run on algorithms you do not control. A post can reach 100,000 people one week and 4,000 the next. Accounts get hacked, shadowbanned, or locked out with no warning. If your artist's whole presence lives on one platform, one bad week can cut off the audience you spent years building.

A website does not have that problem. Nobody can throttle it, nobody can take it down over an algorithm change, and nobody owns the relationship between your artist and the fan except you. The mailing list is yours, the traffic is yours, and the fan who finds the site at 2 a.m. can buy the vinyl, watch the video, and sign up for tour alerts without opening an app.

2. New fans search Google, not Instagram

Think about how a new fan actually finds an artist. A friend mentions a name, a song plays in a cafe, a clip shows up on a timeline, and the fan opens Google and types the name. What comes up decides what that fan learns first.

If the first result is a real website, the fan sees the new album, the tour dates, the videos, and the story, all in one place. If it is a social profile, the fan sees the last few posts and a link in bio, and has to go hunting. Google ranks real websites with real content. It does not rank a link page. Every week without one is a week of new fans forgetting the name by dinner.

3. Everything in one place is what fans actually want

Look at what a complete artist site holds. Take the example site for Maya Sol, a fictional R&B singer. Her album Velvet Hours gets a full page: release date, label, all twelve tracks with lengths, total runtime 45 minutes and 54 seconds. The lead single Golden Hour has its own line, 11 million streams in its first six weeks. Her tour page lists all ten cities with venue, date, and ticket status, including the Detroit homecoming at the Fillmore, already sold out. Her videos page has four videos with real view counts. Her shop has six items: vinyl, a tee, a hoodie, a CD, a tour poster, and a candle.

That is the standard. The band example, The Velvet Static, does the same for four people: member profiles, three albums with full tracklists, a twelve city tour, four videos, and merch printed in small runs. The actor example, Dana Cole, does it for a different career: fourteen screen credits, an awards shelf, a now filming block, three ventures, and four upcoming appearances. One format, three careers, and every one gives a fan or a promoter something real to work with.

4. Promoters, press, and brands judge the site before they judge the artist

When a promoter is deciding whether to book your artist, a journalist whether to write about them, or a brand whether to work with them, they open the website first. They want the same things: the music, the numbers, the tour history, the press quotes, and a way to reach the team. If the site has all of it, your artist looks ready. If it is a link page, that person has to dig through social media, and most of them will not.

A real site is also your artist's press kit. The bio is written, the press quotes are collected in one place, the photos are high resolution and ready to download, and the booking and press emails are right there. Every hour a manager spends assembling that material is an hour a website could have saved.

5. A website works while the artist sleeps

A tour announcement goes out at 6 p.m. and fans are buying tickets at midnight. A new single drops on a Friday and the video is getting watched on Sunday morning. Your artist cannot answer every DM, but a website can: the ticket click, the merch order, the email signup, all at 3 a.m.

A link page can only point somewhere else. A website closes the loop.

The bottom line

A link in bio is a signpost. A website is a destination. If your artist is serious about a career that lasts longer than the next algorithm change, they need a home.

The good news is that a real artist website does not take months. A custom site built for one artist, with the music, the tour, the merch, the story, and the press in one place, is ready in 2 to 4 weeks. Start with the artist page and see the format done properly.
