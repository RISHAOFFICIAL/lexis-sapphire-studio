What Should Be on an Artist's Website? The Checklist Managers Actually Use

Before you approve an artist website, run it against this list. Any missing section is a dead end for fans and promoters.

Managers ask the same question before every launch: is the site actually ready, or does it just look ready? A pretty homepage is not a finished website. A finished one answers every question a fan, a promoter, a journalist, or a brand will ask.

This is the checklist we use before any artist site goes live. Eight sections, each one with a job to do. The examples come from the three fictional artist sites on our artist page.

1. Music, the full catalog, not just the new single

The music section is the heart of the site, and it has to be complete. The new album gets a full page: release date, label, every track with its length, and the total runtime. On the Maya Sol example site, the Velvet Hours page lists all twelve tracks with lengths and the total runtime of 45 minutes and 54 seconds.

  • Every album and EP, with date, label, and track count.
  • Full tracklists with lengths and singles marked.
  • Streaming links included.

2. Tour dates, every date, every venue, every status

A tour page that lists three dates and a link to a ticket site is not a tour page. Fans want the whole run: every city, every venue, every date, and whether tickets are still available. The Velvet Static example lists all twelve cities, from the December 4 hometown show at Saint Andrew's Hall, sold out, through the February 6 closer in Atlanta.

  • Every date with day, venue, and city.
  • Honest ticket status on each date.
  • Doors and show times listed.

3. Videos with real view counts

Videos are proof. An official video, a live cut, and an acoustic session tell a fan more in ten minutes than a bio ever will. Put the view counts on them. A video with 3.7 million views is a credential, and hiding the number wastes it. Maya Sol's page has four videos, including Midnight in the D at 3.7 million views.

  • Official videos, live cuts, and acoustic sessions.
  • View counts shown, not hidden.
  • Length and release date on each.

4. Merch that tells fans what they are buying

Merch is not a link to a store. It is a page that shows the product, says what it is, and tells the fan why it matters. The Maya Sol shop has six items, from plum vinyl pressed in a run of 1,000 to a rose water candle poured in Detroit. Small runs and real details make a fan buy now.

  • Every product with a photo and description.
  • Sizes, formats, and run sizes stated.
  • Tour merch tied to the tour dates.

5. A story with specifics, not a paragraph of adjectives

Most artist bios read like they were written by someone who never met the artist. Three sentences, five adjectives, no facts. A real bio has dates, places, and turning points. A promoter reading it knows who they are booking. A fan reading it has a reason to care.

Maya Sol's story page has a timeline: first open mic at seventeen, first paid gig at nineteen at Baker's Keyboard Lounge, first album recorded at home, Saint Andrew's Hall sold out in 2024, signed that fall.

  • A timeline with real dates and places.
  • The turning points: first gig, first record, first sellout.
  • Where the artist is from, and where they are now.

6. Press quotes with the outlet named

A quote without a source is decoration. A quote with the outlet named is credibility. Collect the best lines, name the publication, and put them where a promoter will see them.

  • Four to six best quotes, each attributed.
  • Quotes tied to specific work.
  • Press and booking emails easy to find.

7. Film, TV, and business, for artists who do more than music

Not every artist is only a musician, and the site has to hold the rest of the career. A manager for a multi-hyphenate client should check this section first, because it is where the rest of the career either shows up or disappears.

The Dana Cole example is an actor and producer, so her site leads with film and television: eight role cards, then the full list of fourteen credits below. Her awards shelf shows three wins and three nominations.

  • Every credit with year, title, role, and type.
  • Awards with the body and the result.
  • Ventures and upcoming appearances with dates.

8. Contact, booking, and social, all in plain sight

The last section is the simplest, and the one most sites get wrong. The booking email, the press email, and the social handles belong where anyone can find them in five seconds. A promoter who has to message a social account to ask how to book your artist has already decided you are hard to work with.

  • Booking email and press email in the footer.
  • Every social handle listed as text.
  • A contact page that says who handles what.

The bottom line

Run your client's site against this list and count the missing sections. One or two gaps is a weekend of work. Five or six is a rebuild, and a rebuild done properly is ready in 2 to 4 weeks.

The checklist is also the fastest way to brief a web designer. Hand them this list, point at the example sites on the artist page, and say: build us this, for our artist.
